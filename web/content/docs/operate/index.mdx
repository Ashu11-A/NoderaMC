## Two services, neither of them in charge

A **tracker** answers one question: who is on this world.
Peers announce themselves to it and query it, and that is the whole of its job.

A **rendezvous relay** does the other half.
Most home connections cannot be dialled from outside, so two peers that both sit behind a NAT need something with a public address to introduce them.
When a direct path can be opened, the relay steps out of the way.
When it cannot, the relay carries an encrypted circuit between the two — and it carries it without being able to read it.

## Why running one is a small decision

Neither service is authority, and that is not a policy — it is enforced by the peers.

Every service proves its own identity before a peer will dial it.
Everything a service says about a world, an identity, a membership or a vote is checked against signatures the peers already hold.
A hostile tracker cannot invent a world, forge a peer, or change what happened in a region.

The most a broken or malicious service can do is **omit**: hide a peer from you, or hand you one that is not reachable.
That degrades discovery.
It cannot corrupt a world, and it cannot make one machine's account of a region beat the committee's.

<Callout type="note" title="Which is why the app takes lists from anyone">
  Because a service cannot lie about anything that matters, there is no need for a curated registry
  and no privileged list.
  The list this project publishes is one store among however many you add, and it is deletable like
  any other.
  <a href="/services/"> The official list</a> is on this site, and
  <a href="/docs/operate/publish-a-service-list"> publishing your own</a> is a JSON file against a
  schema.
</Callout>

## What it costs you

Both are single static binaries with no database behind them.

They hold soft state — who announced what, recently — and they are designed to be restarted, replaced and drained without anyone noticing.
Peers discover services through the lists they have and migrate off one that is draining, so taking yours down is an operation rather than an incident.

There are published container images for both, and the two self-hosting guides on this site are the repository's own — mirrored rather than rewritten, so they cannot drift from what the binaries actually do.

<Callout type="warning" title="One honest caveat">
  Relay fallback across the open internet has not yet been proven at scale by this project.
  It works, it is tested, and the live cross-internet soak is
  <a href="/status"> a task that has not been run</a>.
  Running a relay is genuinely useful to that; it is not a settled, boring piece of infrastructure
  yet.
</Callout>
