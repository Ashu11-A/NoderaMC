Two services keep peers able to find and reach each other. Neither is in charge of anything.

<Scroller>

| | Tracker | Rendezvous relay |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Answers | "Who is on this world?" | "How do I reach that peer?" |
| Listens on | `0.0.0.0:25600`, TCP and UDP | `0.0.0.0:25601`, TCP |
| Holds | Soft state: who announced what, recently | Live circuits, and nothing else |
| Costs you | Almost nothing | Bandwidth — a relayed circuit's traffic goes through you |
| Image | `ghcr.io/ashu11-a/noderamc:tracker-latest` | `ghcr.io/ashu11-a/noderamc:rendezvous-latest` |

</Scroller>

Both are single static binaries with no database behind them. Both drain on `SIGTERM` — refuse new
work, tell the peers, tell the trackers, finish what is in flight — so taking yours down is an
operation rather than an incident. Peers discover services through the lists they hold and migrate
off one that is draining.

The relay only carries traffic it has to. When a direct path can be opened between two peers it
steps out of the way; when it cannot, it bridges an encrypted circuit it cannot read.

<Callout type="tip" title="Pin a digest, not a tag">
  Every push publishes `…-sha-<commit>` alongside the moving `-latest` and `-canary` tags. The
  sha-tagged image is the one to run if you want to know what you are running.
</Callout>

## Why running one is a small decision

Neither service is authority, and that is not a policy — it is enforced by the peers.

Every service proves its own identity before a peer will dial it. Everything a service says about a
world, an identity, a membership or a vote is checked against signatures the peers already hold. A
hostile tracker cannot invent a world, forge a peer, or change what happened in a region.

The most a broken or malicious service can do is **omit**: hide a peer from you, or hand you one
that is not reachable. That degrades discovery. It cannot corrupt a world, and it cannot make one
machine's account of a region beat the committee's.

Which is also why there is no curated registry and no privileged list: because a service cannot lie
about anything that matters, the list this project publishes is one store among however many you
add, and it is deletable like any other. <a href="/services/">The official list</a> is on this site,
and <a href="/docs/operate/publish-a-service-list">publishing your own</a> is a JSON file against a
schema.

## Self-updating, and what it proves

Both services can replace their own binary from a published release. **The update channel is empty
by default**, so nothing happens unless an operator sets one: somebody who chose to run a tracker
did not thereby agree to let it fetch and execute new code.

When it is on, the service compares the published SHA-256 of its own asset against the digest of
the file it is executing — a version string would never change, because the rolling prerelease
republishes under one tag. It verifies before it touches anything, and it drains before it swaps.

It does **not** prove who published the release, because no signing key exists yet: the signature
check is built and tested, and it is waiting on a key that does not exist. Until then the lane
verifies integrity and says so on every check. The gap is confined to a lane you have to switch on,
and it is written down rather than glossed over, as <a href="/status">tracker limitation L-81</a>.

## Start here

<a href="/docs/operate/tracker">Run a tracker</a> is the shorter of the two and the more useful one
to add to the network. <a href="/docs/operate/rendezvous">Run a relay</a> costs more bandwidth.

The two reference pages — <a href="/docs/operate/tracker-reference">tracker</a> and
<a href="/docs/operate/rendezvous-reference">rendezvous</a> — are the complete settings and endpoint
lists, for when you are past the first hour. Both, and the two guides, are the repository's own
documents mirrored rather than rewritten, so they cannot drift from what the binaries do.

<Callout type="warning" title="One honest caveat">
  Relay fallback across the open internet has not yet been proven at scale by this project. It
  works, it is tested, and the live cross-internet soak is
  <a href="/status"> a task that has not been run</a>. Running a relay is genuinely useful to that;
  it is not a settled, boring piece of infrastructure yet.
</Callout>
