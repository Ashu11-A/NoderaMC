## Start with what a server actually does

A Minecraft server does two jobs that have nothing to do with each other.

It **decides what happened** — that the arrow hit, that the piston fired, that the ore is now in your inventory.
And it **stays switched on**, so the world is there when someone wants it.

Almost everything annoying about running a world with friends comes from the second job.
Somebody pays for a machine, somebody has to keep it patched, and when that person loses interest the world goes with them.
The first job is the one you actually need, and it does not require a machine that belongs to anyone in particular.

NoderaMC splits the two.
The deciding is done by whoever is playing, collectively and checkably.
The staying-switched-on is done by a small background process on the machines of people who care about that world, and it does not need Minecraft to be open.

## Regions

The world is cut into a fixed grid of **8×8-chunk regions** — 128 blocks square.

A region is the unit of everything: it is what gets simulated, what gets validated, what gets stored, and what gets handed from one machine to another.
The grid is fixed rather than dynamic on purpose, because two machines that disagree about where the boundaries are cannot agree about anything else either.

Which regions your machine is responsible for follows you.
Your render distance describes a disc around you; the regions that disc touches are the ones you activate.
Walk far enough and the disc moves, so the set is recomputed, and the region behind you is picked up by whoever is still near it.

## Committees

Each active region has a small **committee** of peers, and one of them is the primary.

The primary simulates the region: it takes the inputs for a tick batch and produces a result.
The others in the committee re-execute *the same inputs* and compute the result independently.
Then all of them compare a short fingerprint of the resulting world state — a <Tag>StateRoot</Tag> — and the batch commits only if a quorum of them got the same one.

If they disagree, the batch does not commit.
That is the whole mechanism, and it is why no single machine — including the one that created the world — is trusted to say what happened in it.

<Callout type="note" title="This is why determinism is the project's central bet">
  Re-execution is only a check if two honest machines running the same inputs get bit-for-bit the same answer.
  Minecraft is not written that way, so the parts of the game that have been brought into this lane were rewritten to be.
  That work is not finished, and <a href="/status">the list of what is still outside it</a> is public.
  A region holding something not yet in the validated lane falls back to ordinary vanilla behaviour rather than being validated — it keeps working, it is just not being re-checked.
</Callout>

## Trackers and relays hold no authority

Two machines still have to find each other, and most home connections cannot be dialled directly.

A **tracker** answers "who is on this world".
A **rendezvous relay** introduces two machines that are both behind a NAT and carries an encrypted circuit between them when there is no direct route.

Neither is trusted.
Every service proves its own identity before a peer will talk to it, and everything a service says about a world, an identity or a vote is checked against signatures the peers already hold.
The most a hostile or broken service can do is hide a peer from you, or point you at one that is not reachable.
That is an availability problem, never a correctness one — which is exactly why the app will take a list of services from anyone, and why the bundled list is deletable like any other.

## The worker, and why the mod refuses to start without it

Alongside the mod there is a headless **peer worker**: a small always-on process that is your node on the network.

It holds your identity, keeps your worlds announced, serves pieces of them to peers who ask, and keeps validating regions for worlds you help host.
It runs whether or not Minecraft is open, which is the entire reason your world does not vanish when you quit.

The mod **requires** it.
If nothing answers on <Tag>127.0.0.1:25610</Tag> when the game starts, the mod aborts with an install prompt rather than pretending to work.
The companion app is what supervises the worker, which is why <a href="/docs/start/install">the app is installed first</a>.

## What NoderaMC is not

**It is not a way to play with people who have not installed it.**
Every player in a NoderaMC world runs the mod and joins as a peer.
There is no vanilla-client lane and no second-class participant; the handshake enforces it.
There *is* a separate feature for playing with an unmodified Minecraft — <a href="/docs/start/play-without-the-mod">a tunnel to a host's own LAN world</a> — but that is a different thing wearing a similar hat, and it does not make the guest a peer.

**It is not a hosting service, and there is nothing to sign up for.**
No account, no login, no company holding your world.

**It is not finished.**
The engine, the networking and both services are proven without Minecraft attached; wiring them to a live game world is the largest piece still open, and several parts of Minecraft — redstone, fluids, weather, mobs, lighting — are not all in the validated lane yet.
The project keeps a register per component, every row naming the task that owns it and the test that closes it, and <a href="/status">all of them are on this site</a>.

**It is not a claim that your world can never go down.**
It is a claim that the world does not depend on one particular machine.
Nobody has to stay, but somebody has to be around: a world whose every peer is offline is a world nobody is holding.
